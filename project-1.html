Project study

The New World Screwworm

Assessing what environmental datasets can tell us about conditions relevant to New World screwworm research, while keeping descriptive findings separate from explanations of spread.

Contributor
Brodde Stanford
Workstream
Environmental spatial context and data comparability
Featured analysis period
September 1–30, 2026

The problem

The project asks which environmental conditions could influence transmission, what alternative explanations exist, and what additional information would reduce uncertainty.

A central challenge is deciding whether environmental sources describe comparable locations, reporting periods, and measurements. Regional summaries alone cannot establish conditions at detection sites or explain screwworm movement.

My role

My contribution focused on environmental data collection and preparation, source comparability, and interpretation of descriptive comparisons. I assessed the strength of the findings, considered geographic differences as a possible explanation, and identified information gaps.

Brandon Santor provided source compilation, metadata joins, and contextual guidance. Ethan DeMelo contributed earlier technical intake. Codex assisted with scripts, checks, and draft interpretation; I was the submitting researcher.

The data and method

NASA POWER environmental estimates

The analysis used retained daily NASA POWER estimates for sampled grid centres in Guatemala, Mexico, and Texas. Temperature and relative humidity were summarized as arithmetic means of daily values at each centre. Daily precipitation rates were multiplied by a one-day duration and summed over the stated period.

September summaries used 30 distinct valid dates per group. Separate October summaries covered October 1–3 only and were not treated as a complete month. Missing source values were kept distinct from zero.

The documented checks compared daily inputs with original JSON records, including coordinates, dates, units, and source pointers. Aggregates were reconstructed from the retained values. These checks assessed processing consistency; they did not independently validate actual weather.

Station data usability

The contribution also assessed NOAA SSOD precipitation records and a Guatemala station metadata addendum. Partial reporting intervals, missing records, unmatched station codes, and conflicting metadata were retained as limitations rather than resolved by guessing.

The NOAA review found no eligible full-day or monthly precipitation totals in the assessed records. The Guatemala metadata checks did not resolve observation timing, physical datum, or station relocation questions.

What I found

The sampled Texas grid centres had a higher median temperature and lower median relative humidity in September than the sampled centres in Mexico and Guatemala.

The temperature ranges overlapped. This supports a qualified finding about the medians, rather than a claim that every sampled Texas centre was warmer. These summaries are not regional area averages and do not establish a biological threshold, transmission mechanism, or risk ranking.

The October results covered only three days. Comparing them with a complete September month would not provide a comparison of equivalent reporting periods.

What I would do differently

I would prioritize detection-event records with precise locations and clearly defined dates before assessing whether the regional environmental patterns represent conditions associated with detections.

My second priority would be elevation and climate records linked to the sampled centres, to examine geographic differences as a possible explanation for the temperature patterns.

If environmental estimates matched to detection locations showed a different pattern, I would revise my interpretation of the regional findings’ relevance to screwworm movement. The original sampled regional description could remain valid while being less representative of detection locations.

These are proposed next steps. This contribution did not perform event-weather matching or a causal test. A descriptive association would not, by itself, prove that weather caused spread.
